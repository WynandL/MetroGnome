What a tuning drone is for

A tuner tells you whether one note is exactly right. A drone teaches your ears to keep it right while you play, which is a different skill and the one that actually decides whether you sound in tune in a section, a quartet or a band. When your note sits exactly on the drone, or on a pure interval above it, the two sounds fuse. When it is a few cents off, they beat: a slow pulsing in the loudness that speeds up as the error grows and stops dead when it is gone. Practising against a drone is practising hearing that pulsing and removing it, until the adjustment happens before you have consciously noticed the problem. The full guide to drone practice covers the mechanism and a routine that uses a drone and a tuner together; this page is the drone itself.

How to use this drone

Pick the note. Tap a key on the keyboard, then set the octave. For a scale or a study, choose its key note. For open-string work, choose the string. For long tones, A is the usual pick, because it is what an ensemble tunes to.

Set the reference pitch. A4 = 440 Hz is the standard; set 442 if your orchestra tunes there, 415 for baroque pitch. The drone re-pitches automatically, and every note on the keyboard moves with it, exactly as the reference pitch setting in the app moves both the tuner's target and the drone together.

Start it, then play. Hold your note against the drone and listen for the beating. Slide until it stops, then try to land on the note cleanly the first time. Play slow scales over the key note and listen to every degree settle; play fifths and octaves above it and listen for the interval locking. Stop when your ears tire, which is sooner than you think; ten focused minutes beat a distracted hour.

Check with a tuner afterwards. Not during: a microphone in the same room hears the drone and locks onto it. Stop the drone, then check your note with a tuner you trust. If the ears and the needle agree, the practice is working.

Pure or warm, root or octave

The Pure voice is a single sine wave at exactly the note. It is the cleanest reference there is, but it gives you only one place to hear beating, right at the fundamental, so small errors are easy to miss. The Warm voice adds a stack of harmonics above the note and a gentle shimmer around it, the way a reed organ or a harmonium does. Your own instrument carries harmonics too, so now every pair of harmonics that lands near each other can beat, and a small error shows up in several places at once. Use Warm when you are actually practising intonation and Pure when you want an uncluttered reference.

The Octave blend adds the same note an octave higher at a lower level. It makes the drone fuller and easier to hear against a low instrument, and it is the closest thing here to the layered sound of a tanpura. In the app, a Fifth blend adds a perfect fifth tuned as a pure 3:2 ratio rather than the equal-tempered fifth, which is the beatless interval a string or wind player tunes their own fifths against; the Reed and String voices add brighter and denser harmonic stacks for the same reason Warm helps more than Pure.

Why the note never moves

Everything alive in the Warm voice, the harmonics and the slow shimmer, is built around one component that is never detuned and never moves: a sine at exactly the note frequency. The shimmer comes from quiet copies of the harmonic series a few cents either side of it, breathing slowly so the texture never freezes into one fixed beat rate, but the centre stays put. That is the rule that makes a tone this rich still usable as a pitch reference, and it is the same rule the app's drone is built on. Every harmonic is an exact whole-number multiple of the note, too, because a stretched or bell-like series has no single pitch to tune to.

The reference pitch is the whole game. If the drone is at 440 and your tuner is at 442, the two will disagree by about eight cents and you will chase a difference that is not in your playing. Set the same A4 on both, and if you play with an ensemble, set the A4 the ensemble actually tunes to.

Questions about tuning drones

What is a tuning drone?
A tuning drone is a single sustained note, held steady for as long as you need, that you play or sing against to practise intonation. When your note matches the drone, the two blend into one sound; when it is slightly off, you hear a slow pulsing called beating. The skill is hearing that beating and adjusting until it stops. Cellists, violinists, wind players, singers and brass players all use drones, and Indian classical music has used the tanpura for the same purpose for centuries.
Which note should I set the drone to?
For scales and studies, use the key note (the tonic) of whatever you are playing, so every degree of the scale is tuned against the note it has to resolve to. For open strings, use the string itself: violin G3, D4, A4 and E5; viola C3, G3, D4 and A4; cello C2, G2, D3 and A3; guitar E2, A2, D3, G3, B3 and E4. For long tones, A3 or A4 is the usual choice because it is the note ensembles tune to.
What reference pitch should I use?
A4 = 440 Hz is the international standard and the right default for most players. Many European orchestras tune to 442 or 443 Hz, so set that if you play with one. Baroque ensembles usually use 415 Hz, a semitone below modern pitch. Whatever you choose, use the same reference on the drone and on your tuner, or the two will disagree by a few cents.
Should I use the pure or the warm sound?
Pure is a single sine wave at exactly the note, the cleanest possible reference, but it gives you only one place to hear beating. Warm adds a stack of harmonics above the note, so a small error shows up as beating in several places at once and is much easier to catch. Use Warm for intonation practice and Pure when you want an uncluttered reference and nothing else.
Can I run this drone and a tuner app at the same time?
Not usefully. A tuner listens through a microphone, and in the same room it will hear the drone and lock onto it instead of you, reporting a perfectly in-tune note that is the drone's own. Use the drone to practise by ear, then stop it and check yourself with the tuner. In the Metro Gnome app the tuner needle pauses automatically while the drone sounds, for exactly this reason.
Is this tuning drone free and accurate?
Yes on both counts. It runs entirely in your browser, needs no download, account or payment, and generates the tone continuously from the note frequency rather than looping a recording, so there is no seam and no drift. The centre of the tone is always exactly the note at the reference pitch you set; the warm voice adds harmonics and a gentle shimmer around it but never moves the note itself.
