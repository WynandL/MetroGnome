5/8 Time Signature: How to Count It, How It Differs From 5/4, and a Click to Practise With

5/8 time has five eighth notes in every bar, grouped 2+3 or 3+2 exactly like 5/4, but with a pulse fast enough that you feel only two beats per bar: one short, one long. That short-long limp is the whole meter. The metronome below is set to 5/8 with the 2+3 grouping accented, so you can hear the two uneven beats rather than count five.

What 5/8 time means

The top number says five pulses per bar and the bottom number says the eighth note is the pulse. Five cannot be split into equal twos or threes, so 5/8 is an odd meter grouped 2+3 or 3+2. So far it is identical to 5/4. The difference is entirely about speed and notation: choosing 5/8 over 5/4 tells the player the pulse is quick, quick enough that the bar is felt as two uneven beats (a two-pulse beat and a three-pulse beat) rather than as five countable beats. Nobody counts to five in a fast 5/8; they feel short-LONG or LONG-short.

That is why 5/8 turns up in fast folk dances, in film and game scores that want a nervous energy, and in progressive rock, while 5/4 is used for slower, more spacious fives like Take Five. Same grouping, different gear.

How to count 5/8

2+3: count ONE two ONE two three, accents on pulses 1 and 3. At tempo this becomes a simple short-LONG.

3+2: count ONE two three ONE two, accents on pulses 1 and 4. At tempo, LONG-short.

The best 5/8 practice is to conduct it in two uneven beats: a quick downbeat and a slower second beat (or the reverse). If you can feel a limp rather than a count, you have it.

What 5/8 feels like

5/8 hobbles, on purpose. Two beats of unequal length per bar produce a limp that is impossible to dance evenly to, which is exactly why Balkan dances built around it have such distinctive steps. At speed it is restless and driving; the ear keeps trying to hear a 6/8 lilt and keeps being short-changed one pulse. Composers use it when they want the music to feel unsettled without being chaotic.

Songs and pieces in 5/8

  • Paidushko horo (Bulgarian folk dance): the definitive 5/8, grouped 2+3, a limping dance step that matches the meter exactly.
  • Six Dances in Bulgarian Rhythm (Béla Bartók, from Mikrokosmos): the set that brought Balkan odd meters into the classical repertoire; one of the dances is in 5/8.
  • Fast fives in film and game scores (the whole idiom): when a chase or action cue is in five, it is usually notated 5/8 rather than 5/4 because the pulse is quick.

How to practise 5/8 with a metronome

Feel two, not five. With the click running, tap only the accents (1 and 3 for 2+3) and let the pulses fall between them. The moment you feel a short beat and a long beat instead of five numbers, the meter is yours.

Learn it from 5/4 if that helps. Set the tool to 5/4 at 66 BPM, count all five, then switch to 5/8 at 132 BPM. It is the same bar, twice as fast; hearing that relationship is what makes 5/8 stop feeling like a separate skill.

Push the tempo deliberately. Unlike most odd meters, 5/8 gets easier faster, because at speed the five pulses vanish and only the two beats remain. Start around 100 on the eighth note to hear the pulses, then climb.

Questions about 5/8

Is 5/8 the same as 5/4?
The grouping is the same (2+3 or 3+2) but the beat value is different: an eighth note in 5/8, a quarter note in 5/4. In practice 5/8 signals a fast five felt as two uneven beats, while 5/4 signals a slower five where each beat can be counted, like Take Five.
How do you count 5/8 time?
Group it 2+3 (ONE two ONE two three, accents on 1 and 3) or 3+2 (ONE two three ONE two, accents on 1 and 4). At real tempos you will feel it as two uneven beats, short-long or long-short, rather than counting five.
What does 5/8 feel like?
Like a limp. Two beats of unequal length per bar give it a hobbling, restless drive that is common in Balkan folk dance and in film and game music that needs to feel urgent. The ear keeps expecting a 6/8 lilt and keeps getting one pulse fewer.
