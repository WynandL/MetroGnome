Online Metronome

A free metronome that runs in your browser. Set any tempo from 20 to 300 BPM with the slider, the buttons or by tapping along to a song, pick a time signature, and tap any beat to put an accent on it or take it off. Turn your sound on and press Start.

How to use it

  • Tempo. Drag the slider, press the minus and plus buttons, or use the arrow keys (hold Shift to move in tens). The name under the number is the Italian tempo marking that BPM usually falls under.
  • Tap tempo. Press TAP (or the T key) along with a song, four to six times. The metronome averages your last six taps. Stop for more than two and a half seconds and the next tap starts a fresh count.
  • Time signature. Choose a common meter, or set the number of beats from 1 to 16 and the note value underneath. Beats are drawn in their natural groups, so 6/8 shows as two groups of three and 7/8 as 2+2+3.
  • Accents. Gold beats play a higher click. Tap any beat to switch its accent on or off, for example to accent only beat one, or to mark a 3+2+2 grouping in 7/8.
  • Subdivision. Adds quieter clicks between the beats: 2 for eighth notes, 3 for triplets, 4 for sixteenths.
  • Keyboard. Space starts and stops the click from anywhere on the page.

Your settings are remembered in this browser, so the metronome opens the way you left it. You can also link straight to a setting: this link opens it at 72 BPM in 6/8.

What BPM means, and how to choose a practice tempo

BPM is beats per minute: 60 BPM is one click a second, 120 BPM two a second. Which note gets the beat comes from the bottom number of the time signature, so in 4/4 each click is a quarter note and in 6/8 each click is an eighth note.

A practice tempo is not the tempo of the piece. A useful starting point is the fastest tempo at which you can play the passage cleanly three times in a row without tensing up. Play it there until it feels easy, then move up a few BPM. If a speed-up makes the passage fall apart, the jump was too big; go back to the last clean tempo and take smaller steps. The speed trainer automates exactly this, stepping the tempo up every few bars, and the method is explained in how to practise increasing BPM.

Five ways to practise with a metronome

1. Subdivide when it is slow. Slow tempos are harder to hold than fast ones, because the gap between clicks is long enough to drift in. Switching subdivision to 2 or 3 fills the gap so you can feel where the beat is going. Our article on why you rush when you play explains why slow playing pulls players ahead of the click.

2. Put the click on 2 and 4. In swing, funk and most pop, the backbeat is where the groove lives. Set the metronome to half your tempo in 2/4 and hear each click as beats 2 and 4 of a 4/4 bar, or keep 4/4 and turn the accent off beat 1 and on for 2 and 4. More on this in the guide for jazz musicians.

3. Accent the grouping, not just the one. In 7/8 or 5/4 a single accent on beat one leaves you guessing where the inner groups start. Accent the first beat of every group instead (the default here) and the bar counts itself. The time signature guides cover the common odd meters one at a time.

4. Take the click away. Play along for a few bars, then stop the click and keep going, then bring it back and see whether you are still together. It is the most honest check of your own sense of time, and the steady tempo test measures it for you.

5. Measure, then practise. The rhythm test tells you in milliseconds how close to the click you play and whether you tend to rush or drag, which tells you which of the exercises above to start with.

Is an online metronome accurate?

It depends on how it is built. A web page that simply sets a timer for every click is at the mercy of the browser, which delays timers whenever the page is busy, and the clicks wander audibly. This metronome works the way music software does: a short timer looks a fraction of a second ahead and books each click on the browser's audio clock, the clock the sound card itself runs on, so the spacing between clicks stays steady. The beat lights are fired from the same clock, delayed by the output latency the browser reports, so what you see lines up with what you hear.

Two things are outside any web page's control. Bluetooth speakers and headphones add a fixed delay, often a fifth of a second, which moves every click by the same amount: the tempo is still exact, but the click is late against a recording or another player. And a browser tab left in the background may be slowed by the operating system; for a long session, keep the tab visible. Why phone metronomes drift, and how to test one, is covered in how accurate is your metronome app.

Questions about the online metronome

Is this online metronome free?
Yes. It runs entirely in your browser with no download, account or payment, on a computer, tablet or phone.
How accurate is an online metronome?
This one schedules every click on the browser's audio clock, the same clock that plays the sound, a fraction of a second ahead. That keeps the spacing between clicks steady even when the page is busy. What a browser cannot control is the fixed delay between the computer and your speaker, which is larger over Bluetooth. That delay shifts every click equally, so the tempo stays exact; it only matters if you are lining the click up with something else, such as a recording.
How do I find the BPM of a song?
Press TAP in time with the song, four to six times. The metronome averages your last six taps and sets itself to that tempo. A pause of more than two and a half seconds starts a fresh count.
Why can I not hear the metronome on my iPhone?
On iPhones and iPads, web audio can be silenced by the ring/silent switch. If the beat lights move but you hear nothing, flip the switch to ring and check the volume.
What does the bottom number of the time signature change?
Which note value counts as one beat: a quarter note in 4/4, an eighth note in 6/8. The metronome clicks once per beat of the top number at the BPM you set, so the bottom number does not change the speed of the click. It tells you how the music is written.
