What a polyrhythm is

A polyrhythm is two even pulses sounding at once, each dividing the same span of time a different way. In 3 against 2, one hand plays three evenly spaced notes in the time the other plays two, and both hands start together on the first note, the one. Neither part is a rhythm you would call difficult on its own. The skill is keeping each hand even while the other hand pulls it somewhere else.

The trick is that the two parts share a hidden grid. Three and two both divide evenly into six, so if you count six even pulses, the three falls on 1, 3 and 5 and the two falls on 1 and 4. Played together that makes four sounds per cycle: both hands, then the three, then the two, then the three again. Players have long learned it with a phrase that has the same shape, nice cup of tea: both hands on "nice", the three on "cup", the two on "of", the three on "tea".

What this polyrhythm test measures

You hear the polyrhythm for two cycles, tap along with it for two more, and then the parts drop out, leaving only a click on the one, while you keep both hands going for four cycles. Only those four cycles are scored. The test lays its own grid over your taps, at the tempo and position that fit them best, and asks three questions.

Notes in place counts the notes that landed near where they belong, out of all the notes four cycles hold. A missed note or an extra tap costs points here. Hands on the one is the typical gap between your two hands on the note they share. Off the grid is how far, on average, each tap sat from its place on the grid. Your score multiplies how complete your polyrhythm was by how close it sat to the grid.

Device delay cannot touch this score. The grid is fitted to your own taps, so a speaker, headphone or Bluetooth delay, which moves every tap by the same amount, moves the grid with them. What is scored is how your two hands line up with each other, which no delay can change.

How to get better at polyrhythms

Count the shared grid out loud. Say "1 2 3 4 5 6" slowly and evenly, and tap only the numbers each hand owns. It feels mechanical at first, which is the point: once the combined pattern is in your body you can stop counting and hear it as one rhythm.

Learn each hand alone, then add the other. Tap the slower part until it is automatic, then layer the faster part over it. Swap which hand plays which part once it settles, because the other way round is a different skill.

Slow down until it is clean. A polyrhythm played slowly and evenly is worth more than one played fast and lopsided. The speed trainer can then raise the tempo in small steps, and the steady tempo test checks that your pulse holds without the click.

Questions about polyrhythms

What is a polyrhythm?
Two even pulses played at the same time that divide the same span of time differently. In 3 against 2, one hand plays three evenly spaced notes while the other plays two, and both start together on the first note.
How do I play 3 against 2?
Count six even pulses. One hand plays on 1, 3 and 5, the other on 1 and 4. Together they make the pattern both, right, left, right, which many players learn with the phrase "nice cup of tea".
Does my device's audio delay affect the score?
No. The test fits its own grid to your taps and scores how your two hands line up with each other and with that grid. A speaker or Bluetooth delay moves every tap by the same amount, which changes nothing about how the hands relate.
Is this polyrhythm test free?
Yes. It runs entirely in your browser, needs no download, account or payment, and nothing you tap is uploaded anywhere.
