What this rushing or dragging test measures

Every round starts with four count-in clicks, which set the tempo. A drum groove then carries on from the last click. Over the next two bars its tempo drifts: either it creeps up, which is rushing, or it slows down, which is dragging. Your only job is to say which. Get two in a row right and the next drift is smaller. Miss one and it gets bigger. After 10 rounds the size of the drift settles around the smallest change you can reliably hear, and that is your result.

That makes it a rusher or dragger test for your ears: can you hear a band's tempo slip, the way a bandleader or conductor does in rehearsal? It is the listening half of keeping time, and it is the half most players never measure.

Rushing and dragging: what the words mean

Rushing is the tempo creeping up, so each beat arrives a little sooner than the last. Dragging is the opposite: each beat arrives a little later and the music sags. Neither is a wrong note, which is exactly why they go unnoticed. A passage can have every pitch right and still lurch ahead in the exciting bars and settle back in the hard ones.

Players use the same two words for placement against a click, too: rushing is landing ahead of the beat and dragging is landing behind it. This test is about the first meaning, a tempo that drifts, which is what a listener hears when a band pushes or sags. For placement against a click, use the rhythm test.

The starting tempo changes every round. Between 80 and 150 BPM, picked at random. If every round started at the same tempo you could memorise it and listen for any departure from that one pulse. Moving it around makes every round a fresh judgement against the count-in you just heard, so the result measures your ear rather than your memory of the test.

Why an ear test, and why that is a fair test

A tapping test has to fight your device. The click leaves the speaker late, your tap is registered late, and the two delays are different on every phone and browser. This test sidesteps all of that. You never tap, so output delay shifts every beat by the same amount and leaves the spacing between beats alone, and spacing is the only thing you are judging. It is also why the groove stays free of any on-screen cue: there is nothing to watch, only to hear.

What your result means

The result is the tempo change, as a percentage of the starting tempo, that you can pick out about seven times in ten. Lower is finer. The bands below are a guide set for this test, not a published norm, and one run on laptop speakers in a noisy room can easily read twice as wide as the same ear on headphones.

What research says about hearing tempo changes

In Drake and Botte's 1993 experiments, as summarised in McAuley's 2010 chapter on tempo and rhythm, listeners judging a single time interval needed a change of about 6% to notice it. Given several steady beats to judge by, the threshold fell to about 3% on average, and to just under 2% at best, around 150 BPM. More beats to listen to means a finer threshold, which is why every round here gives you a full count-in and two bars of groove. This test asks a different question from theirs, so treat its bands as a guide rather than a match for those figures. Three habits are worth trying:

  • Run it little and often. Two or three runs a day beat one long session a week, and the after-answer view shows you exactly what the drift looked like.
  • Listen for the feel, not the numbers. A rushing groove feels like it is leaning forward and a dragging one feels like it is settling back. Use the Hear rushing and Hear dragging buttons to learn both.
  • Practise against a click. Playing a passage with a metronome and then without it, and listening back, is how most players first hear their own drift. The guide to why you rush when you play explains where it comes from, and the speed trainer steps a click up and down so you can feel the change.

Hearing it versus playing it

Questions about rushing and dragging

What does rushing or dragging mean?
Rushing means the tempo creeps up: the beats arrive a little sooner each time, so the music pushes ahead of where it started. Dragging is the opposite: the beats arrive a little later each time and the music slows down. Players also use the words for placement against a click, where rushing means landing ahead of the beat and dragging means landing behind it. This test checks whether you can hear the first kind, a drifting tempo.
Where does the phrase "rushing or dragging" come from?
It is ordinary rehearsal language for a band or an orchestra: a conductor or bandleader asking whether a player is ahead of the tempo or behind it. The film Whiplash (2014) made the question famous with a drummer being asked exactly that. This test turns the question around and checks whether you can hear the answer.
What is a rusher or a dragger?
Players tend to lean one way. A rusher drifts ahead of the tempo, often when excited, when playing loudly or fast, or when reaching for a fill. A dragger settles behind it, often when reading or when working through something technically hard. Most people are a bit of both depending on the tempo, which is why this test moves the starting tempo every round.
Is this a rushing or dragging game?
It plays like a short game, 10 rounds in about a minute and a half, but it is scored like a test. Every round is a fresh two-way choice, and the size of the tempo change adapts to you until it settles on the smallest change you can reliably hear.
How can I tell if I am rushing or dragging when I play?
Hearing it in a recording is one skill and feeling it as you play is another. This test measures the first. To measure your own playing, tap along to a click on the free rhythm test, which reports your spread in milliseconds, or run the steady tempo test, which shows whether you speed up or slow down once the click stops.
What is a good score on the rushing or dragging test?
Lower is finer. Under 2 percent is a conductor's ear, 2 to 4 percent is band-ready, and 4 to 8 percent is a good musical ear. Above 8 percent is common on a first go and improves quickly. These bands are a guide set for this test, not a published norm.
Why did I get a different result the second time?
Some variation is normal. A lucky or unlucky guess on a close call can move the result a little, and the starting tempo is different every round. Run it two or three times and take the best: that is closer to what your ear can do than any single run.
Do my speakers or headphones change the result?
Not the way they would in a tapping test. You never tap, so output delay does not matter: every beat is delayed by the same amount and the spacing between them is untouched. Clear sound still helps, so headphones in a quiet room are best, and tiny laptop speakers can blur the groove.
Is the rushing or dragging test free?
Yes. It runs entirely in your browser, needs no download, account or payment, and nothing you answer is uploaded anywhere.
