Shruti box, tanpura, tuning drone: one job, three instruments

A tanpura is the traditional source of the drone in Hindustani and Carnatic music: four long strings, tuned to Pa, Sa, Sa and the lower Sa (or Ma or Ni in place of Pa for ragas that avoid the fifth), plucked one after another in an unbroken cycle. The curved bridge with its threads, the jivari, is what makes the sound bloom with overtones, so the drone is never just a note but a whole shimmering field of harmonics around Sa.

A shruti box is the simpler relative: a bellows and a set of reeds, essentially a harmonium with the keyboard taken away, sustaining a chosen Sa and usually Pa, sometimes the upper Sa as well. It does not cycle or bloom the way a tanpura does; it simply holds. That steadiness, plus a price and a size a student can manage, is why it became the everyday practice drone, and why electronic shruti boxes and tanpura apps followed.

A Western tuning drone is the same idea arrived at from the other direction: a sustained reference note that string, wind and voice students play against to train intonation by ear. Whatever the name, the job is identical. Hold an exact pitch continuously, so that the musician learns to hear every note in relation to it. The drone on this page is a tuning drone set up to behave as a shruti box: a warm, reedy voice on the Sa you choose, with the upper Sa blended in.

Choosing your Sa

Sa is not a fixed frequency. Unlike Western concert pitch, where A is 440 Hz for everyone, each musician chooses the Sa that suits their voice or their instrument, and the whole raga moves with it. Harmonium players name the choice by key colour and position: safed (white) and kali (black), counted from C, so safed 1 is C, kali 1 is C sharp, safed 2 is D, and so on up to kali 4 (G sharp) and safed 6 (A).

If you are choosing for the first time, sing the lowest phrase of a raga you know and the highest, and find the Sa on which both are comfortable. A Sa that is too high strains the top; too low, the lower octave disappears. Once chosen, keep it: a settled Sa is what lets the ear build its map of the swaras.

How to practise with the drone

Start with Sa itself. Sing or play Sa against the drone and listen for the moment the two sounds fuse into one. If you hear a slow pulsing, a wavering in the loudness, you are slightly off; slide until it stops. That pulsing is called beating, and hearing it stop is the entire skill.

Then Pa, then the rest. Hold Pa against the drone and listen for the same fusion, a pure fifth locking in. Then Ma, then each swara of the raga, sustaining each one until it sits. Every note has a place where it stops beating against Sa; that place is the shruti.

Slow alankars over the drone. Sa Re Ga Ma, Re Ga Ma Pa, and so on, slowly, letting each note settle before moving. Speed comes later and comes easily once the ear knows where each note lives.

Keep the drone on for the whole session. Unlike a metronome, which you may switch off for free playing, the drone should sound throughout riyaz. Its whole value is that Sa is always there to return to.

What this drone is and is not. It holds Sa exactly and continuously, which is the part that keeps you in tune, and the Warm voice carries the reed-organ harmonics of a shruti box so that a small error shows up as beating in several places at once. It does not pluck in a cycle or carry the jivari buzz of a real tanpura; those are part of the traditional sound, and a tanpura on the stage is not replaced by this. For practice, the exact, unwavering Sa is what matters.

Sa and Pa together

A tanpura's first string is Pa, and a shruti box nearly always sounds Pa alongside Sa. On a real tanpura the Pa string is tuned by ear until it stops beating against Sa, which puts it at a pure 3:2 fifth, a shade wider than the fifth on a keyboard or harmonium. That pure fifth is why a tanpura sounds so settled and a harmonium drone slightly restless. Reference pitch, if you play with a harmonium

Because Sa is chosen freely, the drone's A4 reference only matters when you have to match an instrument that cannot retune. Most harmoniums are built to A4 = 440 Hz, some slightly higher. Questions about shruti boxes and Sa

What is a shruti box?
A shruti box is a small bellows-driven reed instrument, like a harmonium with the keyboard removed, that sustains one or more chosen notes as a continuous drone. In Indian classical music it holds the singer's Sa, usually with Pa (the fifth) and sometimes the upper Sa, so that every other note can be sung in relation to it. Electronic shruti boxes and apps do the same job with a generated tone.
What is the difference between a shruti box and a tanpura?
Both provide the Sa drone, but a tanpura is a plucked string instrument: four long strings tuned to Pa, Sa, Sa and the lower Sa, plucked in a continuous cycle over a curved bridge (the jivari) that gives the sound its shimmering overtones. A shruti box sustains its notes with reeds and air, so the drone is steady rather than cycling. A tanpura is the richer, traditional sound; a shruti box is simpler, cheaper and easier to keep in tune.
What is Sa, and what note should mine be?
Sa is the tonic of the raga, the home note every other swara is measured from. It is not a fixed pitch like Western concert A; each musician chooses the Sa that suits their voice or instrument. Male vocalists commonly sing with Sa around C, C sharp or D (safed 1, kali 1 or safed 2 on a harmonium); female vocalists commonly around G, G sharp or A; sitar is usually tuned to C sharp or D, and a bansuri is named for its Sa. Pick the note on which your lowest comfortable phrases and your highest both work.
Can a tuning drone replace a shruti box?
For the drone itself, yes: a shruti box, an electronic tanpura and a Western tuning drone all do the same thing, hold an exact reference note continuously so you can practise intonation against it. This page's drone holds Sa with the upper Sa added, like the Sa strings of a tanpura. What it does not reproduce is the plucked cycle and jivari buzz of a real tanpura, which are part of the traditional sound but not what keeps you in tune.
