How Small a Pitch Difference Can You Hear? (And Why Low Notes Are Harder)

Every tuner measures in cents, and a good one reads to a fraction of one. The more interesting question is the one the tuner cannot answer: how many of those cents can your own ear hear? The answer depends on the register, on the sound, on whether the two notes come one after the other or together, and on practice. Each of those is something you can use.

A Cent Is a Ratio, Not a Number of Hertz

A cent is one hundredth of a semitone, and there are 1,200 of them in an octave. The important part is that a cent is a ratio: every cent multiplies the frequency by the same tiny amount, about 1.000578. That matches how hearing works. Your ear judges the step from A3 to A4 and the step from A4 to A5 as the same interval, an octave, even though the first is 220 Hz wide and the second 440 Hz.

Because cents are ratios, the same number of cents is a very different number of hertz depending on where the note sits:

Read it the other way round and you get the reason this article exists. An error of 1 Hz is 2 cents on A5, about 4 cents on concert A, and nearly 16 cents on the open A string of a guitar. The lower the note, the smaller the physical difference that makes it sound out of tune.

What Research Says About the Smallest Difference

The smallest pitch change a listener can reliably detect is called the just noticeable difference, and it has been measured for well over a century. The classic method is the one the pitch ear test on this site uses: play two tones one after the other and ask which was higher, then narrow the gap until the listener is guessing.

Reference figures vary from study to study, because the result depends on loudness, note length, the listener and the method. The Springer Handbook of Speech Processing summarises it like this: below about 500 Hz, the smallest audible change is roughly 3 Hz for a pure sine tone and roughly 1 Hz for a complex tone, the kind real instruments make. Above about 1,000 Hz it settles at around 0.6% for a sine tone, which is about 10 cents.

Convert those hertz figures into cents and the register effect jumps out:

Note3 Hz (pure tone)1 Hz (instrument-like tone)
A2, 110 Hzabout 47 centsabout 16 cents
A3, 220 Hzabout 23 centsabout 8 cents
A4, 440 Hzabout 12 centsabout 4 cents

Two practical conclusions follow. First, a rich sound is far easier to judge than a plain one, because every harmonic carries the same error and the ear can compare all of them. That is why a bright, buzzy reference tone is easier to tune against than a pure beep. Second, judging pitch purely by ear gets harder the lower you go, which is something bass players and guitarists tuning their low strings run into constantly.

These are averages, not limits. Trained listeners routinely beat the reference figures, and untrained listeners often start well above them. Where you start matters much less than how quickly the number moves, which is the next section.

Fine Pitch Hearing Is Trained, Not Inherited

The most encouraging study in this area was published in 2006 by Christophe Micheyl and colleagues in the journal Hearing Research. They compared 30 classical musicians with 30 people who had no musical training. At the start, the non-musicians' thresholds were more than six times wider than the musicians'. The musicians barely improved with extra training, which suggests years of playing had already taken them close to the best the ear can do.

Then a second group of non-musicians trained for 14 hours in total, and somewhere between four and eight hours in, their thresholds matched those of the classical musicians. The gap between a first-time listener and a trained musician is real, and it closes in hours of focused practice rather than years.

That matters for how to read your own result. A first run of a pitch test measures where your ear is today, on that pair of headphones, at that time of day. It says very little about where it can be in a few weeks.

Two Notes Together Reveal Far More Than Two Notes Apart

Everything above is about notes heard one after the other, where your memory has to carry the first note across the gap. Play the two notes at the same time and a different mechanism takes over: beating. Two tones that are slightly apart drift in and out of step, and the combined sound swells and fades at a rate equal to their difference in hertz. The guide to practising intonation with a drone covers this in detail.

Beating turns a pitch difference into a rhythm, and rhythm is something the ear tracks very precisely. A difference of 5 cents on concert A is 1.27 Hz, so the sound pulses about once a second, which nobody misses. Even 2 cents, far below what most people can hear between separate notes, beats about once every two seconds on A4. This is why tuning against a reference that is sounding at the same time is so much more accurate than tuning from memory.

It also explains a trick guitarists and bassists use on low strings. On the open A string, 5 cents only beats about once every three seconds, slow enough to blur into the natural decay of the note. But each harmonic beats faster than the one below it: the second harmonic carries twice the hertz difference, the fourth four times. Tuning with the harmonic at the 12th fret, which sounds an octave higher, doubles the beat rate and halves the time it takes to hear it. The low register is hard on the ear, so move the comparison up.

How In Tune Is "In Tune"?

If the ear can be trained down to a few cents, it is fair to ask how close musicians actually need to be. Music answers that in a more interesting way than a single number.

  • Some intervals are fussy and some are forgiving. On a piano tuned in equal temperament, a perfect fifth is 700 cents wide, only about 2 cents away from the beatless fifth of 702 cents. A major third is 400 cents, almost 14 cents wider than the beatless third of 386. A listener with a 5-cent ear can hear that difference in a held chord, and choirs and string quartets often adjust their thirds for exactly this reason.
  • Vibrato is wide on purpose. Solo singers' vibrato usually swings by less than a semitone either side of the note, while choir singers keep it to a tenth of a semitone or less. The ear hears the centre of the wobble as the pitch, so what matters with vibrato is where it is centred, not how wide it swings.
  • Context moves the target. A note that sounds perfect on its own can sound sour against a chord, and the same note can need a slightly different placement in a different key. This is why the tuning drone exercise of playing a scale over a held root is so useful: it teaches you where each note wants to sit, not just where the tuner puts it.

So the honest answer is that finer is always useful, but a working ear somewhere in the 10 to 25 cent range is already enough to tune by ear, to hear when a note is off centre, and to fix it. Everything below that is refinement.

A Ten-Minute Routine for a Sharper Ear

The Micheyl study points to one thing above all: short, regular, focused practice with immediate feedback. A routine that fits into a practice session:

  • Two minutes: measure. Take the pitch ear test once, on headphones, in a quiet room. Note the number. Using the same headphones each time keeps the results comparable.
  • Three minutes: tune by ear, then check. Detune one string or note slightly, bring it back by ear against a reference, and only then look at your tuner. The look after the guess is the feedback that trains you, so do not peek early.
  • Three minutes: listen for beats. Hold a note against a drone or a sustained reference and slowly bend it in and out of tune. Notice the pulse speed up as you move away and slow down and stop as you arrive.
  • Two minutes: go low. Repeat the tune-and-check step on your lowest string or lowest comfortable note, using the octave harmonic if you play a string instrument. This is the register where the ear is weakest, so it is also where practice pays most.

Retake the test once a week rather than every day, and take the best of three runs. Single results wobble with fatigue and luck, but the trend over a month is reliable, and it is usually more encouraging than people expect.

Common Questions

What is the smallest pitch difference a human can hear?
It depends on the register, the sound and the listener. Standard reference figures put it at roughly 3 Hz for a pure tone below 500 Hz, about 1 Hz for an instrument-like tone, and around 0.6%, or about 10 cents, for a pure tone above 1,000 Hz. Trained musicians often hear finer than that, and heard together rather than one after the other, much smaller differences become audible as beating.
How many hertz is one cent?
It depends on the note, because a cent is a ratio rather than a fixed number of hertz. One cent is about 0.25 Hz at concert A (440 Hz), about 0.06 Hz on a guitar's open A string (110 Hz), and about 0.51 Hz at A5 (880 Hz).
Why is it harder to tune low notes by ear?
Because the ear's smallest audible change in hertz stays roughly the same at low frequencies, while a cent shrinks to a tiny fraction of a hertz. The same out-of-tune amount in cents is a much smaller physical difference on a low note, and its beating is slower and easier to lose in the note's decay. Comparing harmonics an octave or two higher makes the error easier to hear.
Can adults improve their pitch hearing?
Yes. In a 2006 study by Micheyl and colleagues, adults with no musical training started with thresholds more than six times wider than classical musicians and matched them after roughly four to eight hours of training. Short, regular practice with immediate feedback works best.
How close to in tune do I need to be?
Closer is always useful, but an ear that hears 10 to 25 cents is already enough to tune by ear and to hear when a note is off centre. Some intervals are less forgiving than others: an equal-tempered fifth is only about 2 cents from a beatless fifth, while an equal-tempered major third is about 14 cents wide of a beatless one, which a trained ear can hear in a held chord.
Is hearing small pitch differences the same as perfect pitch?
No. Perfect pitch is naming a note with no reference at all. Hearing small differences between two notes is the finest form of relative pitch, and it is the skill that tuning by ear and playing in tune with others actually rely on. Unlike perfect pitch, everyone can improve it with practice.
