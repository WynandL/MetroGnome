Time Signatures Explained: Simple, Compound and Odd Meters

A time signature tells you how many beats are in a bar, which note value carries the beat, and, if you know where to look, how the bar is meant to feel. This guide explains the two numbers, the three families every meter belongs to, and then goes through the twelve time signatures you will meet most often, with how to count each one and music to hear it in.

The quick answer. Look at the top number only. 2, 3 or 4: simple, each beat splits in two. 6, 9 or 12: compound, each beat splits in three, and you feel the top number divided by three. Anything else (5, 7, 11...): odd or irregular, counted in uneven groups of twos and threes. The bottom number never changes which family a meter is in. Jump to the chart of common time signatures.

What the two numbers mean

The top number is how many beats are in each bar. The bottom number is which written note value counts as one beat: 4 is a quarter note, 8 an eighth note, 2 a half note. So 4/4 is four quarter-note beats per bar and 3/8 is three eighth-note beats.

The bottom number is notation, not feel. A piece written in 3/8 and one written in 3/4 can sound identical; only the note values on the page differ. That is why the family a meter belongs to depends on the top number alone.

Simple time: beats that split in two

When the top number is 2, 3 or 4, each beat divides naturally into two halves. 2/4 is simple duple (two beats, the march), 3/4 is simple triple (three beats, the waltz), and 4/4 is simple quadruple, so common it is called common time. Count simple time with "and" between the beats: "1 and 2 and 3 and 4 and". If that subdivision feels natural, you are in simple time.

Compound time: beats that split in three

When the top number is 6, 9 or 12, the bar is built from groups of three. A bar of 6/8 holds six eighth notes, but you do not feel six beats; you feel two, each made of three. Divide the top number by three to find the beats you feel: 6/8 is felt in two, 9/8 in three, 12/8 in four.

This is why 6/8 is not two bars of 3/4. Both hold six eighth notes, but 3/4 is three groups of two (ONE two THREE four FIVE six, felt as a waltz) and 6/8 is two groups of three (ONE two three FOUR five six, felt as a lilt). Clap a bar and ask whether you hear three groups of two or two groups of three; the grouping, not the note count, is the meter.

Odd and irregular meters: grouping is everything

Some top numbers do not split into equal twos or threes. 5/4, 7/8 and 11/8 are counted in uneven groups: 7/8 is most often 2+2+3, so the bar sounds like "ONE two ONE two ONE two three". Each group starts with an accent, and the longer group is what gives these meters their lurch.

"Odd meter" strictly means the top number is odd (5, 7, 11, 13). Meters like 8/8 or 10/8 that are grouped the same uneven way are better called irregular or asymmetric, since their top number is even. Either way, the grouping is a choice the music makes: the same 7/8 can be 2+2+3, 3+2+2 or 2+3+2, and the accents tell you which.

6/8

Two beats per bar, each rolling in three: the jig, the tarantella, the slow rock ballad. While learning, count all six with weight on 1 and 4; at tempo, feel just two. If it feels like three beats, you are in 3/4. Heard in The House of the Rising Sun (The Animals), Metallica's Nothing Else Matters and Irish jigs. Hear 6/8

9/8

Three beats of three: a waltz that swings. Count ONE two three FOUR five six SEVEN eight nine and feel three. Heard in Debussy's Clair de Lune and in slip jigs. Dave Brubeck's Blue Rondo à la Turk regroups the same nine as 2+2+2+3, a different meter in practice. Hear 9/8

12/8

Four beats, each with a triplet inside: the frame of 4/4 with a roll underneath. It is the sound of slow blues, shuffles and fifties ballads. Heard in Led Zeppelin's Since I've Been Loving You and Unchained Melody (The Righteous Brothers). Hear 12/8

5/4

Five beats, grouped 3+2 or 2+3; at speed you feel a long beat and a short one. Say the numbers with the accent on 1 and 4 (or 1 and 3) before you play. Heard in Take Five (The Dave Brubeck Quartet, 3+2), the Mission: Impossible theme and Holst's Mars. Hear 5/4 as 3+2

5/8

The same five at a quick pulse, felt as two uneven beats, a limp rather than a count. It is common in Balkan dance, such as the Bulgarian paidushko horo (2+3), and in Bartók's Six Dances in Bulgarian Rhythm. Hear 5/8 as 2+3

7/4

Seven beats slow enough to count each one, usually felt as a bar of four and a bar of three, so the downbeat arrives a beat early. Heard in Pink Floyd's Money (4+3) and Peter Gabriel's Solsbury Hill. Hear 7/4 as 4+3

7/8

Seven quick pulses in three groups: 2+2+3 (short-short-long) is the most common, 3+2+2 (long-short-short) the next. At tempo you count the three groups, never seven. Heard in the Bulgarian ruchenitsa (2+2+3), the Greek kalamatianos (3+2+2) and Radiohead's 2 + 2 = 5. Hear 7/8 as 2+2+3

11/8

Eleven pulses, never counted to eleven: group them as 4+4+3 (the easiest way in), 3+3+3+2, or the Balkan 2+2+3+2+2. Heard in the intro riff of Whipping Post (The Allman Brothers Band, 3+3+3+2) and the Bulgarian kopanitsa. Hear 11/8 as 3+3+3+2

Accents make a meter audible

A click that plays seven identical sounds for a bar of 7/8 gives you nothing to hold on to. What helps is an accent on the first pulse of every group: for 7/8 as 2+2+3, pulses 1, 3 and 5. The same applies to simple and compound time: an accent on 1 and 4 makes 6/8 sound like two beats instead of a flat run of six. When you practise, set your metronome's accents to the grouping of the piece, not just to beat one.

How to learn a new time signature

  1. Count it out loud, away from your instrument, until the numbers come without effort.
  2. Clap the accents, loud on the first pulse of each group and light on the rest.
  3. Start slow, at a tempo where you can place every accent on purpose.
  4. Speed up only once the grouping feels automatic.
  5. Add the instrument last, keeping the same accents, so the shape you learned carries over.

Questions about time signatures

How do I tell if a time signature is simple or compound?
Look only at the top number. 2, 3 or 4 is simple: each beat splits in two. 6, 9 or 12 is compound: each beat splits in three, and you feel the top number divided by three (6/8 is felt in two). Anything else, such as 5, 7 or 11, is an odd or irregular meter, grouped in uneven twos and threes. The bottom number never changes the class.
Is 9/8 simple or compound?
Compound. 9/8 is compound triple: three beats per bar, each made of three eighth notes, counted ONE two three FOUR five six SEVEN eight nine. Some music, such as Blue Rondo à la Turk, regroups the nine as 2+2+2+3, which is an odd grouping of the same nine pulses.
Is 3/8 simple or compound?
Simple. 3/8 is simple triple, three eighth-note beats per bar, the same structure as 3/4 written in shorter note values. At fast tempos it is often felt as one beat per bar.
Is 6/4 simple or compound?
Compound. 6/4 is compound duple, like 6/8 but written with quarter notes: two beats per bar, each split into three. Composers occasionally use 6/4 for three beats of two instead, and then the music, not the signature, tells you which; by the standard rule 6/4 is compound.
What is the difference between 3/4 and 6/8?
Both bars hold six eighth notes, but 3/4 groups them as three beats of two and 6/8 as two beats of three. 3/4 is a waltz, ONE two THREE; 6/8 is a jig or a lilt, ONE two three FOUR five six with the weight on 1 and 4.
How do you count 7/8?
Count the groups, not seven even pulses. The most common grouping is 2+2+3: ONE two ONE two ONE two three, with accents on pulses 1, 3 and 5. Some music uses 3+2+2 (accents on 1, 4 and 6) or 2+3+2 (1, 3 and 6).
