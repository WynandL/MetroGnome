Why You Rush When You Play (And Why It Gets Worse at Slow Tempos)

Almost every musician has been told they rush. Very few have ever been told by how much, in which direction, or at which point in the bar. That missing detail is the difference between a vague criticism you carry around for years and a habit you can actually fix in a fortnight.

Rushing and dragging get talked about as if they were personality traits. Some players are "rushers", some are "draggers", and that is apparently that. It is a useless way to think about it, because it turns a measurable, correctable habit into a fixed character flaw. Timing is not a gift. It is a motor skill with a number attached, and once you know your number the fix stops being mysterious.

Start By Measuring, Not Guessing

Before any of the advice below is worth reading, get a baseline. You cannot judge your own timing while playing, for reasons worth understanding in their own right, and a teacher's "you were a bit ahead there" is a memory of a feeling rather than a measurement.

Our free rhythm test takes about twenty seconds in your browser. Tap along with the click for sixteen beats and it reports two separate things: how tightly your taps clustered, and your average offset, which is the number this article is about. A negative offset means you played ahead of the beat. A positive one means you played behind it. Note the direction and the size before you read on, because the fix for a consistent 70 ms rush is nothing like the fix for taps scattered randomly by 70 ms in both directions.

Two different problems, two different fixes. A consistent offset in one direction is a tempo-perception habit, and it responds to deliberate correction: you lean the other way until the new placement feels normal. Random scatter in both directions is a motor-control problem, and leaning does nothing for it. It responds to slower, cleaner repetitions. Treating one as if it were the other is why so much metronome practice produces so little change.

Why Rushing Happens

Rushing is not carelessness. It is what your body does under a fairly predictable set of conditions, and naming them tells you where to look.

  • Arousal speeds up your clock. Adrenaline compresses your sense of time. A tempo that felt correct in the practice room genuinely feels slow under stage lights, so you "correct" it upward without noticing. This is why bands so often play their set faster live than on the record.
  • Effort pulls the pulse forward. Loud passages, fast passages and technically demanding ones all tighten the body, and physical tension shortens note values. Anywhere you are working hard is a place to suspect rushing.
  • Anticipation runs ahead of execution. You already know what comes next, and knowing what comes next makes you reach for it. The bar before a chorus, the bar before a fill, the run-in to a solo: these are not random spots, they are the spots where you are thinking ahead.
  • Comfort tempos hide the habit. Most players gravitate to a narrow band of tempos where everything feels natural. Practising there almost exclusively means the habit never gets tested. It shows up the moment a piece sits outside that band.
  • Drummers accelerate into fills specifically. Moving the hands into position for a fill takes real time, and the usual unconscious solution is to start the fill fractionally early and speed up through it. This one is so consistent that it is worth practising grooves and fills as separate exercises before ever joining them.

Dragging has a shorter list. It usually comes from either reading and processing (you are decoding the notation rather than playing it) or from physically not being able to execute a passage at the tempo, in which case the timing problem is really a technique problem wearing a disguise.

Why Slow Tempos Are Harder Than Fast Ones

Here is the part that surprises people. If your timing falls apart somewhere, it is far more likely to be at 60 BPM than at 160.

Fast tempos are propped up by information. At 160 BPM a beat arrives every 375 milliseconds, so you are corrected almost continuously and there is very little room in each bar for drift to accumulate before the next reference point arrives. There is also so much happening per bar that a small timing error is buried under the sheer density of notes.

At 60 BPM you get a full second of silence between beats, and nothing in that second tells you where you are. You are running on your internal clock alone, which is exactly the thing being tested. Worse, the maths works against your perception: 50 milliseconds of error is 13 percent of a beat at 160 BPM, where it is unmissable, but only 5 percent of a beat at 60 BPM, where it feels almost fine. The error is identical in milliseconds. It just stops feeling like an error.

The practical consequence is straightforward. Slow practice is not a gentler version of fast practice, it is a harder and more revealing version, and it is the tempo range where a timing habit has nowhere to hide. If you can hold a genuinely steady pulse at 60 BPM you can hold one anywhere.

Five Fixes That Actually Change the Number

Every one of these is aimed at a specific cause above, and every one is testable. That last part matters: if you cannot re-measure, you cannot tell a real improvement from a good day.

  • Subdivide out loud. The single highest-value change most players can make. Counting only the beat leaves the gap between beats as guesswork, and guesswork is where drift lives. Count or vocalise the eighths, then the sixteenths. The gaps stop being empty and the pulse stops wandering. This is also why a metronome that can click subdivisions is worth more than one that only marks the beat.
  • Practise where you fail, not where you are comfortable. Set the metronome to the tempo that exposes the habit, which is usually slower than you would choose. If your comfort zone is 120 BPM, your problem is at 70.
  • Isolate the transitions. Loop the two bars around the spot where you accelerate: the run-in to the fill, the last bar before the chorus, the entry after a rest. Play those four bars against a click twenty times. Timing rarely fails evenly across a piece, so practising the whole piece to fix one corner is mostly wasted repetition.
  • Deliberately overcorrect, then settle. If you know you sit 60 ms ahead, spend a session aiming to play noticeably behind the click. It will feel wrong, which is the point: your sense of "on the beat" is the thing that is miscalibrated. Aim past the target and let it settle back.
  • Use a descending tempo ramp. Everyone builds speed with an ascending BPM ramp, few ever practise downward. Working from 120 down to 60 in structured steps trains the exact skill that fails you, and a speed trainer that can descend as well as ascend makes it automatic instead of something you have to remember to set up.

Then Measure Again

Re-run the same test after a week of this and compare two numbers. The average offset tells you whether the directional habit is shifting toward zero. The spread tells you whether your control is genuinely tightening or whether you have simply traded a consistent rush for random scatter, which is a common and easily missed outcome of overcorrecting.

One caveat worth keeping in mind: on a browser, or on any device, part of your measured offset is the hardware rather than you. Sound takes time to leave a speaker and a tap takes time to register, and that delay shifts every reading in the same direction. It is why the direction and the change in your offset are more trustworthy than its absolute value, and why serious measurement corrects for device latency before reporting anything. It is also worth knowing that not every metronome app keeps perfect time in the first place. A wandering reference makes any measurement taken against it meaningless.

Common Questions About Rushing and Dragging

Why do I rush when I play?
Rushing is usually arousal plus effort. Adrenaline speeds up your internal sense of time, so a tempo that felt right in the practice room feels slow on stage. Physical effort adds to it: loud, fast or technically demanding passages tighten the body and pull the pulse forward. It is a habit produced by conditions, not a lack of natural rhythm, which is exactly why targeted practice moves it.
Is it worse to rush or to drag?
Rushing is more noticeable because it compounds. Every early note shortens the gap to the next one, so a small acceleration builds through a section. Dragging tends to be self-limiting, since the effort of playing behind pulls you back toward the beat. Neither is fatal. What matters more is whether the error is consistent, which is correctable by leaning the other way, or scattered, which needs slower and cleaner repetitions instead.
Why is playing slowly harder than playing fast?
At 60 BPM there is a full second between beats and nothing in that gap tells you where you are, so you are running on your internal clock alone. At 160 BPM the beats arrive often enough to correct you constantly. Slow tempos also make each error a smaller fraction of the beat, so it feels less wrong while being just as wrong in milliseconds.
How do I know if I am rushing or dragging?
Measure it, because you cannot feel it reliably while playing. Take a rhythm test that reports your average offset in milliseconds along with the direction, or use a metronome app that listens through the microphone and scores where your notes landed relative to the click across a whole session.
How do I stop rushing the tempo?
Subdivide out loud so the gaps between beats stop being guesswork, practise at the slow tempos that expose the habit rather than the comfortable ones that hide it, isolate the transitions where you accelerate such as the bar before a fill or a chorus, deliberately overcorrect until the new placement feels normal, and re-measure after each change.
Does practising with a metronome fix rushing on its own?
Not reliably. A metronome gives you a reference but not a measurement, so you can play along with one for years while still landing consistently early and never knowing it. The click has to be paired with feedback about where your notes actually landed, which is the difference between practising time and improving it.
