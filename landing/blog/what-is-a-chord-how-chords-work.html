What Is a Chord? How Chords Work, Why They Matter, and How to Name Any Chord You Play

Every song you love is a handful of chords in a particular order. Every riff you have ever worked out, every harmony line you have sung and every bass part you have played lives inside those chords, and so does the reason a chorus lifts and a verse settles. This is a guide to what a chord actually is, physically and musically, how the names work, what an arpeggio has to do with it, and why being able to name the chords you play changes how you hear, learn and write music. It ends with a free chord finder you can use right now.

Here is a small experiment. Play a C on any instrument. Now play an E above it at the same time. Now add a G. Somewhere between the second note and the third, something changed: you stopped hearing notes and started hearing a thing, a single bright sound with a mood of its own. That thing is a chord, and the rest of this article is about why it happens, what to call it, and what you can do with it.

What a Chord Actually Is

A chord is three or more different notes sounding at the same time, heard as one object. The simplest and most common kind is the triad: a note we call the root, a note a third above it, and a note a fifth above it. Stack C, E and G and you have a C major triad. Stack A, C and E and you have A minor. Almost every chord you will ever meet is one of these with something added, taken away or rearranged.

"A third above" means two letter names up, counting the one you start on: C, D, E. "A fifth above" means four letters up: C, D, E, F, G. Chords are built in thirds because that is the interval the ear fuses most readily into a single sound; stack notes a second apart and you get a cluster, stack them a fourth apart and you get something open and hollow that jazz pianists love but that most listeners hear as unresolved.

The important subtlety is that a third comes in two sizes. From C to E is four semitones, a major third. From A to C is three semitones, a minor third. Which one sits directly above the root is the single biggest decision in the chord, and it is where the words major and minor come from. The fifth above the root is seven semitones in both cases, a perfect fifth, and it is the same in a major and a minor chord. Only the third moves.

The one-semitone rule. C major is C, E, G. Lower the E to E♭ and you have C minor. One note, one semitone, and the entire emotional weight of the chord turns over. Try it on the chord finder: tap C3, E3 and G3, then move the E down a fret.

Why a Major Third Sounds Bright: the Physics

This is the part most chord guides skip, and it is the part that makes the rest make sense. A note on any real instrument is not one frequency. A guitar string vibrating at 110 Hz (an A) is also vibrating at 220, 330, 440, 550 and 660 Hz at the same time, at decreasing loudness. These are its harmonics, whole-number multiples of the fundamental, and their exact mix is what makes a guitar sound like a guitar and a clarinet sound like a clarinet.

Now look at what those harmonics are, as notes. 110 Hz is A. 220 is A an octave up. 330 is E. 440 is A again. 550 is C♯. 660 is E. The fourth, fifth and sixth harmonics of a single A string are A, C♯ and E, which is an A major triad. A major chord is not an invention; it is already inside every note you play. When you sound A, C♯ and E together, their harmonics line up with harmonics that were already there. That agreement is what your ear reports as brightness, stability and "home".

The ratios are what matter. The perfect fifth is a frequency ratio of 3:2, the major third is 5:4, and a full major triad in pure tuning is 4:5:6. A minor triad is 10:12:15: the same fifth, but a third that sits in a more complex ratio, further from the harmonic series of its root. It still fuses, but with a shadow over it. That shadow is what we call sad, dark or serious, and it is the same physics that makes a tuning drone useful: when two notes share a simple ratio their harmonics coincide, and when they are slightly off those harmonics beat against each other, a slow pulsing you can hear and remove.

One caveat for the engineers: the piano and the frets on your guitar use equal temperament, which splits the octave into twelve exactly equal steps so that every key is equally usable. In that system the major third comes out about fourteen cents wider than the pure 5:4, and the fifth about two cents narrower than 3:2, so an equal-tempered major chord always beats very slightly. Choirs, string quartets and brass sections, which are not locked to frets, drift toward the pure ratios without being told to, because that is where the beating stops. If you have ever wondered why a barbershop chord "rings" in a way a keyboard chord does not, that is the answer.

The Chord Families, Briefly

Every chord name is a root plus a description of what is stacked on it. Here are the families you will actually meet, from the ground up. The dictionary behind Metro Gnome's Chord Finder has thirty-eight of these; you do not need all of them tonight, but it helps to see the shape of the whole map.

Power chordRoot and fifth, no thirdC5, E5Neither major nor minor. Rock guitar, distortion, riffs. Strictly an interval, but everyone calls it a chord.
TriadsRoot, third, fifthC, Cm, Cdim, CaugThe four basic moods: bright, dark, tense, strange. Most songs are triads.

Ninths and beyondA seventh chord with the 9th, 11th or 13thC9, Cmaj9, Cm11, C13Richness. Funk lives on the dominant ninth; the 7♯9 is the Hendrix chord in Purple Haze.

Three ideas cover most of what happens in that table. The third decides major or minor. The seventh adds pull. A dominant seventh chord (C7 is C, E, G, B♭) contains a tritone between its E and its B♭, the most unstable interval in the system, and the chord wants to move to F to resolve it; that single fact is the engine of the blues, the reason a V chord leads home to I, and the backbone of the ii V I progression that most of jazz is built on. Everything above the seventh is colour. A ninth, eleventh or thirteenth changes how rich the chord is far more than where it wants to go.

The Bass Note Decides: Inversions and Slash Chords

Take the four notes C, E, G and A. If C is the lowest note you are playing C6, a major chord with a sixth added. If A is the lowest note you are playing Am7, a minor seventh chord. Same four notes, two correct names, and any musician would agree on which is which just by hearing what is on the bottom. The lowest note anchors the ear; it tells you what everything else is measured from.

This is why a chord finder that only takes letter names cannot give you a straight answer, and why Metro Gnome's works with real notes in real octaves. It is also why slash chords exist. C/E means "C major, with E as the lowest note". That is a C chord in first inversion (its third in the bass); C/G is second inversion (its fifth in the bass). And a chord can sit over a note that is not in it at all: F/G, an F major chord over a G bass, is a gospel and pop staple precisely because the bass says "dominant" while the chord above says something softer.

For bass players this is the whole job description. The note you choose under a guitarist's chord is not decoration; it can rename the chord. For pianists it is what the left hand is for. For guitarists it is the difference between the open C shape and the same shape with the G on the low E string added, which is C/G and sounds bigger for a reason.

Why the Names Are Spelled the Way They Are

Here is a thing that trips up nearly everyone, including a lot of chord apps. The third of a C♯ major chord is not F. It is E♯. Same pitch, same key on the piano, different name, and the name is not pedantry.

A chord tone's letter comes from its degree: a third is two letters above the root, a fifth is four, a seventh is six. The root is C♯, so the third is some kind of E, and the pitch it needs to land on is the one usually called F, so in this chord it is written E♯. Write it as F and the chord reads C♯, F, G♯ on paper: a root, a fourth and a fifth, the spelling of a sus4 chord with no third in it, attached to a sound that plainly has one. No arranger, copyist or theory teacher would accept it, and it stops making sense the moment you try to read it on a stave. The same logic gives the seventh of B♭7 as A♭ (not G♯) and the seventh of D♭7 as C♭ (not B).

A small engineering aside, because it is the kind of thing this blog exists for. When we built the Chord Finder in the Metro Gnome app, the chord dictionary was written in degrees ("1 ♭3 5 ♭7"), not in semitone offsets ("0 3 7 10"). The semitone version is simpler to code and every hobby chord namer uses it, and it cannot spell. It knows that a note three semitones above C♯ exists; it does not know that it is an E. The degree version derives the letter from the root and the accidental from the gap, so E♭ major spells itself E♭, G, B♭ and Bdim7 spells its doubly-flattened seventh as A♭. It even gets the octave number right when the letter crosses an octave line: the C♭ in a D♭7 chord played at the pitch B3 is written C♭4, because scientific pitch notation numbers the letter, not the piano key. That was a real bug in an early build, invisible until a chord that spells C♭ came up, and the test suite now checks for it.

What an Arpeggio Is, and Why It Matters More Than It Sounds

An arpeggio is a chord played one note at a time instead of all at once. The word is Italian, from arpeggiare, to play on a harp, and it describes exactly the ripple you hear when a harpist runs a hand across the strings of a chord. Every chord can be played as an arpeggio and every arpeggio outlines a chord, which makes it the bridge between the two halves of music: harmony (the chords) and melody (the line).

Once you start looking, arpeggios are everywhere. A fingerpicking pattern is an arpeggio. A walking bass line is a chain of arpeggios with passing notes between them. A bebop solo is arpeggios with the corners rounded off. The left hand of most piano accompaniment is arpeggios, and so is the intro of nearly every ballad you have ever slow-danced to. If chord shapes are the nouns of music, arpeggios are the verbs: they are how you move through a chord rather than sit on it.

They are also the honest way for a machine to hear a chord, which is why the Chord Finder in the Metro Gnome app listens for them. A tuner's pitch detector (ours uses the McLeod pitch method, the same algorithm behind the tuner's accuracy) works by finding the one period that a waveform repeats at. A single note has one. A strummed chord has three or four at once, superimposed, and there is no single period to find; anything that claims to name a strummed chord from a phone microphone is either guessing from a fuzzy spectral template or being generous with the truth. Play the chord as an arpeggio instead, and each note arrives alone, settles, gets captured exactly, and the chord is named from what was actually heard. It is slower than a strum by about a second, and it is right.

Why This Matters to You, Whatever You Play

Guitarists. You almost certainly learned chords as shapes first, which is fine, but a shape is stuck in one place and a name travels. Knowing that your open D shape is D major, and that D major is D, F♯, A, is what lets you find it at the fifth fret, the tenth, and on a piano. It is what makes the CAGED system make sense, what turns a capo from guesswork into arithmetic, and what lets you hear that the chord you stumbled onto by moving one finger is a Dsus2 or a Dmaj7 and deserves a place in a song. Guitar is also where extended chords hide in plain sight: the everyday "jazz" voicing of a seventh chord leaves out the fifth entirely, because the third and the seventh carry it, and a good chord finder still names it (C7, no 5) rather than shrugging.

Pianists and keyboard players. You have the clearest view of any of us, ten fingers and every note laid out in a row, so chord literacy pays off fastest here. Naming what your left hand does is what lets you read a lead sheet instead of a full score, comp behind a singer from a chord chart, and voice a chord in four different ways without changing what it is. It is also the fastest route to understanding why C6 and Am7 feel like cousins: they are the same notes.

Bassists. Your notes are the ones that name the chord. The root, the fifth, the third, a passing seventh, a chromatic approach from below: every choice you make is a choice of chord tone, and knowing which one you are on is the difference between a bass line that supports and one that merely thumps. Slash chords are your instructions.

Singers and choir members. A harmony part is a chord tone that moves, and when you know which tone you are on (the third, usually, if the melody has the root) your line stops being a list of pitches to memorise and becomes something you can hear. It also explains why your section locks in when it is in tune and shimmers when it is not: you are singing the pure 5:4 or 3:2 ratio, or you are not.

Songwriters and producers. Naming the chords in a progression is what lets you write it down, transpose it for a singer, hand it to a session player, and steal from it on purpose. The reason a chorus lifts is usually a IV chord or a vi; the reason a bridge feels like a journey is usually a secondary dominant; the reason a song sounds like the 1960s is often a ♭VII. None of that is available to you until the chords have names.

Drummers. Yes, you too, and not as an afterthought. A song's form is its chord changes, and a drummer who can hear where the changes are is the one who lands the fill in the right bar, sets up the chorus, and knows the turnaround is coming without counting. The best drummers hear harmony; naming the chords in a tune you are learning is the fastest way to start.

Ukulele players, mandolinists, banjo players. Four strings means your chords are already voiced without a fifth or a doubled root, and a chord finder that understands that is the one that will name what you are actually playing.

Teachers and students. A chord finder that shows the degree next to every note (R, 3, 5, ♭7) is a theory lesson that runs itself. Tap a chord, read the degrees, move one note, read them again.

How Practising Chords Makes You Better

There is a common idea that theory is the opposite of feel, that knowing the names gets in the way of the music. It is exactly backwards, and here is the mechanism.

Ear training that sticks. Every time you name a chord you have just played, you attach a label to a sound. Do that a few hundred times and the labels start arriving on their own: you hear a song on the radio and know it is going to the IV chord before it does. That is not a gift; it is a lookup table you built.

Learning songs in a fraction of the time. A song remembered as shapes is forty separate facts. The same song remembered as "I, V, vi, IV in G" is one fact, and it is the same fact as a few thousand other songs. Names compress.

Transposition for free. The singer wants it a tone lower. If you know the chords as names and functions, you move everything down a tone in your head. If you know them as shapes, you are back at the start.

Improvising that means something. Chord tones are the notes that sound "right" over a chord; the arpeggio is your map of them. Every improviser, from a blues guitarist to a bebop saxophonist, is playing arpeggios with decorations, and knowing the chord is knowing the arpeggio.

Writing, and stealing well. Once you can name the chords in the songs you love, you can borrow the move, not the song. That is how everyone who has ever written anything good did it.

A Practice Routine for Chord Literacy

Name what you already play. Take your five most-used shapes and put their notes into the chord finder. Read the name, the full name, and the degree next to each note. Most people find at least one surprise in their first five.

Move one note. From any chord you know, raise or lower one finger by a fret or a key and name the result. This is how sus chords, added ninths and major sevenths get discovered, and it is the fastest way to grow a vocabulary that is yours rather than a book's.

Reverse-engineer a song. Work out the bass note of each chord by ear, then find the chord over it. Put the notes in, read the name, write it down. Do a whole song and you have learned more theory than a month of flashcards, because every chord came with a context.

Play it as an arpeggio, in time. Chord literacy is not finished until it lives in your hands at tempo. Set a metronome to something slow, play each chord of a progression as an arpeggio from the bass up, and change on the bar. Then use a tempo trainer to walk the speed up in steps rather than in one jump; changing chords cleanly at speed is a timing skill as much as a fretting one, and it is the same skill a speed trainer is built to develop.

Hear the chord tones against the root. Set a tuning drone to the root of a chord and play each chord tone against it. The fifth will lock, the major third will lock with a shimmer, the minor third will sit a little darker, and the seventh will pull. Now you know what those degrees sound like, not just what they are called.

Get to twenty-five. The twelve major triads and the twelve minor triads make twenty-four. Naming twenty-five distinct chords is a real milestone, and in the Metro Gnome app it is the one that earns Metro his acoustic guitar. It was chosen because it is roughly the point at which the names stop being something you look up and start being something you know.

Common Questions

What is a chord in music?
A chord is three or more different notes sounding at the same time, heard as one unit rather than as separate notes. The simplest and most common chords are triads, built from a root note, a third above it and a fifth above it. Which third you use, major or minor, decides most of the chord's character. Two notes played together are an interval rather than a chord, although guitarists call a root and fifth a power chord.
What is the difference between a major and a minor chord?
One note, moved one semitone. A major triad is root, major third (four semitones up) and perfect fifth (seven semitones up). A minor triad lowers the third by one semitone. That single step is the difference between C major (C, E, G) and C minor (C, E♭, G), and between the bright, settled sound and the darker one. The fifth is the same in both.
What is an arpeggio?
An arpeggio is a chord played one note at a time instead of all at once, from the Italian arpeggiare, to play on a harp. Every chord can be played as an arpeggio and every arpeggio outlines a chord. Arpeggios are how melody and harmony meet: a bass line, a fingerpicking pattern, a jazz solo and a piano accompaniment are all built from them. They are also the way a single-note pitch detector, such as the one in a tuner app, can hear a chord.
What is a slash chord?
A chord written as C/E is a C major chord with E as its lowest note, the note after the slash being the bass. Slash chords name inversions (the chord's own third or fifth in the bass) and chords played over a different bass note entirely. The bass note matters because it changes how the chord sounds and, in some cases, what it is called: C, E, G and A are C6 with C in the bass and Am7 with A in the bass.
Why should I learn chord names if I can already play the shapes?
Because a name is portable and a shape is not. A chord you can name you can play in any key, on any instrument, from any chart, and explain to the bass player. Names also carry function: knowing that a chord is the V7 of the key tells you where it wants to go, which is what lets you predict a song you have never heard, improvise over it and write your own. Shapes get you through one song; names get you through every song.
How can I find out what chord I am playing?
Work out the notes and feed them to a chord finder.
